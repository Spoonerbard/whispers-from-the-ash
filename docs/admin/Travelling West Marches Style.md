**I. The Grid Map & Travel Costs**

- **Home Base:** The campaign starts in [[npcs]] (or multiple home bases if you prefer), which acts as a safe haven for the players.
- **Grid-Based Exploration:** The map is divided into a grid, with each square representing a specific area or region.
- **Directional Exploration:** Each session, the players choose a direction (north, south, east, west, or diagonals) and explore the corresponding section of the grid.
- **Known vs. Unknown:** Initially, only the squares adjacent to the home base are known. As players explore, new squares are revealed.
- **Travel Cost:**
    - **Adjacent to Town:** Exploring squares directly adjacent to a town or settlement has no cost.
        
    - **Increasing Cost:** For each additional square traveled beyond the adjacent squares, there's an increasing cost in hit points:
        
        - **1 Square Away:** 1d4 hit points per character.
        - **2 Squares Away:** 1d6 hit points per character.
        - **3 Squares Away:** 1d8 hit points per character.
        - **4 Squares Away:** 1d10 hit points per character.
        - **5 Squares Away:** 1d12 hit points per character.
        - **6 Squares Away:** 1d20 hit points per character
        - **7+ Squares Away:** 1d100% (percentage) of the character's maximum hit points.
    - **Cost is per Character:** Each character pays the cost individually, representing the wear and tear of travel.
        
    - **Cost is Paid Upfront:** The hit point cost is paid _before_ the party sets out on their expedition. This represents the challenges of the journey itself.
        
    - **Minimum Cost:** Even if a character has resistance or immunity to the type of damage, they should still take at least 1 point of damage, this represents spending time on the expedition and using up resources.
        
- **Exploration Time:**
    - It takes roughly one work week to travel to an adjacent square.
    - Each additional square adds another work week of travel time.
    - Fully exploring and mapping a square takes about a work week.
    - Players can choose to spend more time exploring a square to uncover more secrets or search for specific resources. This could take multiple work weeks depending on what they are searching for.
- **Supply Lines:**
    - The distance to a square also affects the time it takes for trade and supplies to travel to and from that location.
    - If players want to send resources or messages back to town from a distant square, it will take longer to arrive. This could also affect the availability of goods or services in that square.
- **Mapping:**
    - Players are responsible for mapping their explorations.
    - Accurate maps can be sold or traded in town for rewards or information.
    - Incomplete or inaccurate maps will be less valuable.
- **Transportation:**
    - Certain forms of transportation, such as flying carpets or teleportation circles, could reduce or negate travel costs, but these should be rare and valuable.

**II. Long Resting in the Wasteland**

- **Risky Affair:** Long resting outside of a safe haven is dangerous and should not be taken lightly.
- **Interruption:** There's a chance that a long rest in the wilderness will be interrupted by a hostile encounter. You can determine the probability based on the location (e.g., a higher chance in more dangerous areas).
    - **Example:** Roll a d100 at the start of each long rest. On a roll of 1-25 (or higher, depending on the location), the rest is interrupted.
- **No Hit Point Recovery:** Long resting in the wilderness does _not_ restore hit points. This represents the lack of proper rest and the dangers of the environment. Hit dice can still be spent as normal.
- **Exhaustion:** If a long rest is interrupted or if the party forgoes a long rest, characters risk gaining levels of exhaustion.
- **Spell Slot Recovery:** Spell slots are still recovered on a long rest, representing the ability to regain magical energy even in harsh conditions. This is assuming the players can create a safe enough space to complete the long rest.
- **Safe Havens:** Certain locations within a square might offer a safer place to rest (e.g., a hidden cave, an abandoned building, a fortified ruin). Finding these locations could be a key part of exploration. You can determine if these locations allow hit point recovery based on their condition.
- **Creating Camps:** Players can attempt to create their camps. Doing so requires time, effort, skill checks, and resources.
    - A successful camp reduces the chance of interruption, provides some hit point recovery on a long rest (e.g., half of the character's maximum hit points), and offers other benefits depending on the resources used.
    - Failure means the camp offers little to no protection and might even attract unwanted attention.

**III. Other Considerations**

- **Resource Management:**
    - **Food and Water:** Track food and water consumption, especially on longer expeditions. [[the Mournland]] might have limited sources of clean water or edible plants.
    - **Carrying Capacity:** Limit the amount of gear and supplies characters can carry, forcing them to make choices about what to bring.
    - **Equipment Degradation:** Tools and equipment could degrade with use, especially in the harsh environment.
- **Disease and Hazards:** [[the Mournland]] could be home to strange diseases or environmental hazards that affect characters' health and resources.
- **Random Encounters:** In addition to planned encounters, use random encounter tables to add an element of unpredictability to exploration. These encounters could be tailored to the specific terrain and distance from the home base.
- **Reputation:** The party's actions and discoveries could affect their reputation in [[npcs]] and with other factions in the region.
- **Factions:** Introduce factions with their own goals and motivations. The party's choices could affect their relationship with these factions, opening up new opportunities or creating new challenges.
- **Cartography Tools:**
    - Players can purchase or craft tools to aid in mapping, such as compasses, sextants, and specialized cartography kits.
    - These tools can provide bonuses to skill checks related to navigation, mapping, and determining the distance and direction of locations.
- **Rumors and Information:**
    - Players can gather rumors and information in town that might hint at the location of valuable resources, hidden settlements, or dangerous areas.
    - The accuracy and reliability of rumors can vary, leading to potential rewards or red herrings.

**IV. Example: Journey to a Location 3 Squares Away**

1. **Cost:** Each character pays 1d8 hit points upfront.
2. **Travel Time:** The journey takes 3 work weeks. The players must bring enough food and water for this period (or find ways to replenish their supplies along the way).
3. **Exploration:** Once they reach the square, they can spend a work week (or more) exploring it.
4. **Long Rest:** If they long rest in the wilderness, there's a significant chance of interruption, and they won't regain hit points without a well-made camp.
5. **Return Trip:** The journey back to the home base will take another 3 work weeks and cost another 1d8 hit points per character.

**Adapting the System:**

- **Difficulty:** Adjust the hit point cost, the chance of long rest interruptions, and the frequency of random encounters to fine-tune the difficulty.
- **Terrain:** Different terrain types could have different travel costs or risks. For example, crossing a mountain range might be more dangerous than traveling through plains.
- **Character Abilities:** Certain class features, spells, or magic items could mitigate the costs of travel or make long resting safer.
- **Narrative Focus:** Use these mechanics to reinforce the themes of your campaign. If you want to emphasize the harshness of [[the Mournland]], make travel costly and long rests perilous. If you want to focus more on exploration and discovery, you can ease up on the costs.
